The Invention of the Transistor

The first working transistor was demonstrated on 16 December 1947 at Bell
Telephone Laboratories in Murray Hill, New Jersey. The device was built by
John Bardeen and Walter Brattain, working in the solid-state physics group
led by William Shockley. Their first device was a point-contact transistor:
two closely spaced gold contacts pressed onto a slab of germanium, a
semiconductor element. When a small current flowed into one contact, it
modulated a much larger current flowing to the other, so the device could
amplify electrical signals.

Shockley, initially excluded from the point-contact breakthrough, responded
within weeks by conceiving the junction transistor, a more robust sandwich
of n-type and p-type semiconductor layers. The junction design proved far
easier to manufacture reliably and became the basis of the semiconductor
industry. Bell Labs publicly announced the transistor in June 1948, and the
name "transistor" — a contraction of "transfer resistor" — was suggested by
John R. Pierce.

The transistor replaced the vacuum tube in most electronics because it was
smaller, generated far less heat, used less power, and was much more
reliable. Early commercial applications included hearing aids and portable
radios; the Regency TR-1, released in 1954, was the first mass-produced
transistor radio. Silicon later displaced germanium as the dominant
transistor material because silicon devices tolerate higher temperatures
and silicon dioxide forms an excellent insulating layer.

For the invention of the transistor, Bardeen, Brattain, and Shockley shared
the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1956. Bardeen would later win a second
physics Nobel in 1972 for the theory of superconductivity, making him the
only person to win the physics prize twice. By the 1960s transistors were
being integrated by the thousands onto single chips, launching the
integrated-circuit era and the exponential scaling described by Moore's
law.
